The Roman aqueducts were among the most impressive feats of ancient engineering. They carried fresh water from distant springs and mountains into cities, often across many miles of difficult terrain. Engineers relied on a gentle, continuous downward slope rather than pumps, since the whole system worked by gravity alone. Even a small error in the gradient could mean the difference between a working aqueduct and a useless one, so surveyors used tools like the chorobates to keep the incline precise over enormous distances.

Where the land dropped away sharply, builders raised the channel on tall arched bridges instead of letting it follow the ground. These bridges are the structures most people picture when they hear the word aqueduct, but they were only a small fraction of the total length. Most of an aqueduct's route ran underground, in covered channels or tunnels, protected from contamination and from the weather. Only the sections that had to cross a valley needed the dramatic arches.

Once the water reached a city, it did not simply flow into every house. It arrived at a large distribution tank, and from there it was divided among public fountains, bathhouses, and a limited number of private connections, usually reserved for wealthy households who paid for the privilege. Public fountains were the main source of drinking water for ordinary residents, who carried it home in jars.

Maintaining an aqueduct was a constant task. Mineral deposits built up inside the channels over years of use, gradually narrowing them and slowing the flow, so crews had to periodically scrape the walls clean. Leaks, collapses, and even deliberate tapping by nearby landowners were persistent problems that required a dedicated administration to police the system and keep the water moving toward the city it served.
